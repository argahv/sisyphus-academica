Recent advances in large language models have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide range of natural language processing tasks. However, these models exhibit a persistent tendency to generate factually incorrect statements, commonly referred to as hallucination. This paper investigates the relationship between model scale and hallucination frequency across three families of transformer-based architectures. We find that while larger models achieve higher accuracy on standard benchmarks, they also produce more fluent but factually ungrounded completions, creating a reliability paradox that existing evaluation metrics fail to capture.

Our analysis reveals that hallucination patterns are not uniformly distributed across model outputs. Specifically, we identify that approximately 37% of hallucinated statements occur in contexts requiring multi-hop reasoning, where the model must compose information from multiple sources within its training data. We attribute this to a fundamental limitation in the attention mechanism's ability to maintain referential coherence across long reasoning chains. To address this, we propose a structured retrieval-augmented generation framework that decomposes multi-hop queries into atomic subquestions, each verified against a curated knowledge base before composition. Our method reduces hallucination rates by 62% on the FEVER dataset while maintaining competitive fluency scores. These results suggest that retrieval-augmented architectures, rather than further scale increases, represent a more promising direction for building reliable language models.
